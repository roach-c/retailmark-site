---
title: What a line review actually decides
date: 2026-08-26
description: A line review is not a sales meeting. It is the one date a category's assortment gets set, and most suppliers walk in prepared for the wrong conversation.
---

Most first-time suppliers prepare for a line review the way they would prepare for a sales meeting. They rehearse the story, polish the deck, bring the product. Then they sit down and discover the buyer is not deciding whether the product is good. That was settled before the meeting was scheduled.

A line review decides what the modular looks like for the next cycle. It is an assortment decision, and assortment is a fixed number of slots.

## The number of slots does not change because your product is good

A category has a set amount of shelf. The modular is drawn to fit it. So the real question in the room is not "is this worth carrying" but "what comes out to make room for it."

That reframes the whole conversation. You are not arguing that your item deserves a place. You are arguing that it earns more per slot than something already sitting there. Those are different arguments and they need different evidence.

If you cannot name what your item displaces, you have not finished preparing.

## Everything hangs off one date

The review date itself is the least useful date to work from. What matters is the chain behind it:

- when materials are due to the buyer, which is usually weeks before the meeting
- when the modular resets in stores, which is usually months after it
- when the first order would ship, which decides whether your capacity is real or theoretical

Work backwards from the reset, not forwards from today. A plan that arrives after the materials deadline is not a late plan. It is not a plan, because it missed the only door.

## Being unprepared is a specific thing, not a vague one

"Unprepared" usually gets used to mean the deck was thin. In practice it means one of a short list of concrete gaps:

- **Item data that will not pass setup.** Case pack, dimensions, weights, UPCs. If the item cannot be built, the yes is worthless.
- **A forecast the buyer cannot check.** A number from your spreadsheet is a claim. A number from the systems the category already uses is a fact.
- **No answer on capacity.** "How many can you ship in week one" is not a hard question, but it is asked in the room and guessed answers get remembered.
- **No read on the competitive set.** If you do not know the velocity of the items around you, you cannot make the displacement argument at all.

## "Not this year" is not always a no

Categories run on a cycle. A buyer who says no in one review is not closing the door on the next one, and the useful thing to leave with is a clear read on which it was.

The way to find out is to ask what would have to be true next time. A specific answer means come back. A vague one usually means the category is not looking for what you have.

Either way you now know something you did not know walking in, which is more than most suppliers get out of the meeting.
